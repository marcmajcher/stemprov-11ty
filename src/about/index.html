Hi! I'm Nic Bennett.

I believe **how we work together shapes the world we build together.** Throughout my career—as a scientist, researcher, organizer, facilitator, and artist—I've returned to the same question: **How do we create spaces where people can practice the world they're trying to build?** Whether I'm facilitating a retreat, designing a leadership program, developing a science communication curriculum, or organizing researchers in defense of science as a public good, my work is grounded in a simple belief: lasting change doesn't come from better information alone. It comes from stronger relationships, shared purpose, and opportunities to practice new ways of leading, learning, and working together. That's why I design participatory, creative, and deeply relational learning experiences that help people build trust, courage, and the collective capacity to act.

An interdisciplinary path

My work sits at the intersection of science communication, community organizing, learning design, and applied theatre. I began my career studying ecology before earning a Ph.D. in science communication, where my research explored power, ideology, identity, and belonging in science communication spaces. Alongside that work, I became increasingly involved in community organizing and found a home in improvisation and Theatre of the Oppressed. Each tradition transformed how I think about learning. Research taught me to ask good questions and ground my work in evidence. Organizing taught me that leadership grows through relationships, shared analysis, and collective action. Applied theatre taught me that people don't just learn by thinking differently—they learn by **rehearsing** different ways of relating to one another. Today, my work brings those traditions into conversation.

From neutrality to solidarity

My scholarship asks what science might become if it were accountable to the communities it claims to serve. As a researcher, I've studied power, ideology, and belonging in science communication. As the founder of Science in Solidarity, I've worked alongside scientists who are learning what it means to show up not only as experts, but as neighbors, organizers, educators, and participants in democratic life. Those experiences have convinced me that many organizational challenges aren't simply technical problems—they're relational ones. How do people build trust across differences? How do they cultivate leadership instead of relying on a handful of exhausted people? How do they move from shared values to shared action? Those are the questions that shape every workshop, retreat, and consulting engagement I design.

What it's like to work with me

Every engagement is built collaboratively around your goals, your people, and your context. My facilitation blends science communication research, learning science, popular education, community organizing, improvisation, and Theatre of the Oppressed to create spaces where people actively engage through dialogue, reflection, storytelling, embodied practice, and creative experimentation. Participants often tell me my workshops feel different—not because they're entertaining (though they're often playful), but because they create the conditions for honest conversation, meaningful participation, and genuine connection.

Why this work matters

I don't see facilitation as simply helping people have better meetings. I see it as helping people practice democracy. Every workshop is an opportunity to experiment with new ways of relating—to share power more intentionally, listen more deeply, navigate complexity with curiosity, and build cultures where more people can contribute and lead. The spaces we create today shape the organizations—and the futures—we're capable of building tomorrow.

Let's build something together

If you're looking for someone to facilitate thoughtful conversations, design memorable learning experiences, or help your team build stronger relationships while tackling meaningful challenges, I'd love to connect. Together, we can create spaces where people don't just learn about change—they practice it.
